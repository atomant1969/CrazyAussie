Core Principle

Every video should have a clear promise, deliver that promise without wasting time, then send the viewer to the next useful step.

The structure is:

  1. Lead-in: Why this video matters and what the viewer will get.
  2. Content: The actual teaching, review, demonstration, or story.
  3. Lead-out: What to do next, which related video to watch, and how this connects to the larger channel.

Beginner Teaching Formula

Use this formula when the topic is practical:

  1. Fear/problem: Name the thing the beginner is worried about.
  2. Simple answer: Tell them the practical answer.
  3. Demonstration: Show the exact steps.
  4. Mistake: Show what beginners usually do wrong.
  5. Fix: Show the better way.
  6. Next step: Send them to the next video in the workflow.

Example:

Problem: You recorded a video on Android but do not know how to get it onto your PC.
Simple answer: Use a USB cable first because it is the most reliable beginner method.
Demonstration: Unlock phone, choose file transfer, open DCIM, copy clips to project folder.
Mistake: Do not send the file through a messaging app that compresses it.
Next step: Organize the footage before editing.

Standard 10-Minute Tutorial Structure

Use this for beginner videos such as Android recording, phone-to-PC transfer, and editing tutorials.

0:00-0:20: Direct Hook

Tell the viewer exactly what problem the video solves.

Example:

If you want to start YouTube with only an Android phone, this video shows the minimum phone specs, the recording settings, and the simple workflow to get your first video ready for editing.

0:20-0:45: Credibility And Scope

Briefly explain what will and will not be covered.

Example:

This is for beginners. No expensive camera, no studio, no complicated setup. Just a phone, a way to transfer footage, and a computer for editing.

0:45-1:00: Outcome Preview

Show or describe the final result.

Example:

By the end, you will know whether your phone is good enough, which settings to use, and how to avoid the mistakes that ruin beginner videos.

1:00-8:30: Main Content

Break the lesson into clear numbered steps.

Suggested pattern:

  1. What you need.
  2. What settings to use.
  3. How to do the task.
  4. Common beginner mistake.
  5. Better way to do it.
  6. Quick recap.

Keep each section focused on one job. If a section grows too large, it should probably become its own video.

For beginner workflow videos, prefer 4-6 clear steps over 15 tiny steps. Too many steps makes a simple task feel harder than it is.

8:30-9:20: Practical Recap

Repeat the workflow in simple order.

Example:

So the basic path is: record short test clips, check audio, transfer the files to your PC, organize them into folders, then start editing.

9:20-10:00: Lead-Out

Give the viewer the next step.

Example:

Next, watch the phone-to-PC transfer video, because recording is only useful if you can actually get the footage into your editing software.

Add a short channel bridge only where natural:

I am using this same workflow while building this channel before the Siberian homestead project begins.

Software Review Structure

Use this for CapCut, DaVinci Resolve, Clipchamp, Shotcut, Kdenlive, and similar videos.

Lead-In

  1. State who the software is for.
  2. State the question being answered.
  3. State the verdict will come at the end.

Example:

Today I am testing whether CapCut Desktop is good enough for a new YouTuber making proper long-form videos, not just Shorts.

Content Body

Use the same categories every time so viewers can compare reviews.

  1. Cost.
  2. Computer requirements.
  3. Beginner difficulty.
  4. Importing footage.
  5. Cutting mistakes.
  6. Audio tools.
  7. Text and captions.
  8. Export settings.
  9. Biggest strength.
  10. Biggest weakness.

Optional scoring:

  • Beginner ease: 1-5.
  • Good for Shorts: 1-5.
  • Good for long-form: 1-5.
  • Low-spec PC friendly: 1-5.
  • Would I use it for this channel: yes/no/maybe.

Lead-Out

Give a clear recommendation.

Example:

If you want the easiest start, use this. If you want more control and are willing to learn, watch the DaVinci Resolve review next.

Android Beginner Structure

Use this for the first beginner videos.

Lead-In

Answer the fear immediately:

Yes, you can start YouTube with an Android phone. The question is whether your phone meets the minimum basics and whether you know how to record clean footage.

Content Body

  1. Minimum phone specs.
  2. Storage and battery.
  3. Camera settings.
  4. Landscape vs vertical.
  5. Lighting.
  6. Audio.
  7. Stability.
  8. Test recording.
  9. Check the footage.
  10. Next step: transfer to PC.

Lead-Out

Move them to the next video in the chain:

Once you can record a clean test clip, the next step is getting that file from your phone to your PC without losing it or making a mess.

Phone-To-PC Transfer Structure

Lead-In

State the practical problem:

Recording is only step one. If your footage stays trapped on your phone, you cannot build a reliable YouTube workflow.

Content Body

  1. USB-C cable transfer.
  2. Phone unlock and file mode.
  3. Finding the DCIM/Camera folder.
  4. Copying files to a project folder.
  5. Naming folders.
  6. Checking playback on the PC.
  7. Backing up important footage.
  8. Common mistakes.

Lead-Out

Send viewers to editing:

Now that the files are on your PC, the next step is choosing editing software and making the first simple cut.

Upload And Publishing Structure

Use this after the export tutorials.

Lead-In

State the point clearly:

Exporting the video is not the end. You still need to upload it properly, choose the thumbnail that appears in the video list, set the title and description, and check the video after publishing.

Content Body

  1. Open YouTube Studio.
  2. Upload the exported video file.
  3. Add title.
  4. Add description.
  5. Choose or upload thumbnail.
  6. Add playlist if relevant.
  7. Set audience.
  8. Choose visibility: private, unlisted, scheduled, or public.
  9. Publish or schedule.
  10. Check the video page and video list after upload.

If filming this as a screen tutorial, zoom or crop enough that viewers can read the YouTube Studio interface on a phone screen.

Thumbnail Section

Explain the difference:

  • Auto-generated thumbnail: YouTube picks frames from the video and lets you choose one.
  • Custom thumbnail: You upload a separate image file.

Beginner advice:

  • If using an auto-generated thumbnail, choose the clearest frame with the subject visible.
  • If using a custom thumbnail, keep it readable when small.
  • Check the thumbnail on mobile because most viewers will see it small first.

Mention account verification when relevant: YouTube says verified accounts can access features such as custom thumbnails and uploads longer than 15 minutes.

Lead-Out

Move the viewer to the next step:

Once the video is published, the next skill is reading the first basic numbers in YouTube Studio so you know whether the title, thumbnail, and topic worked.

Shorts Structure

Shorts should usually teach one idea only.

0:00-0:02: Hook

Say the useful point immediately.

Example:

New YouTubers: clean your phone lens before you record.

0:02-0:20: Demonstration

Show the problem and the fix.

0:20-0:30: Result

Show the before/after or final takeaway.

0:30-0:40: Next Step

Point to the related long video if useful.

Example:

I explain the full Android setup in the longer video.

Channel-Building-In-Public Structure

Use this for videos about the 50k subscriber attempt, analytics, lessons learned, and strategy.

Lead-In

State the experiment clearly.

Example:

I am trying to build this channel to 50,000 subscribers before the Siberian homestead documentary begins. This is what I am testing first.

Content Body

  1. What the goal is.
  2. What was tried.
  3. What worked.
  4. What failed.
  5. What the analytics suggest.
  6. What changes next.

Lead-Out

Invite viewers into the experiment.

Example:

If you are building your own channel, follow along and compare your results with mine.

Later Documentary Structure

Use this once the Siberian build becomes the main content.

0:00-0:30: Situation

What is happening today?

0:30-1:30: Plan

What is the job, why does it matter, and what constraints exist?

1:30-45:00: Work Sequence

Show the actual work. Use clear natural sound, short explanations, and visible progress.

45:00-55:00: Problem Solving

Show what went wrong, what changed, and why.

55:00-60:00: Result And Next Step

Show the result and set up the next episode.

Lead-In Rules

  • Do not start with a long greeting.
  • Do not explain the whole channel history first.
  • Do not ask people to subscribe before giving value.
  • Do tell viewers the exact problem being solved.
  • Do show or promise a concrete result.
  • Do start with the viewer's problem, not your channel biography.

Lead-Out Rules

  • Do not ramble.
  • Do not end with five unrelated calls to action.
  • Give one next video or one next action.
  • Ask for comments only when the answer will help future videos.
  • Mention the Siberian project briefly when it naturally connects.
  • Point to only one next video when teaching a step-by-step beginner sequence.

Simple Ending Formula

Use this when unsure:

If you are starting from zero, do this step first. Then watch the next video on [next topic], because that is where the workflow continues.

Quality Check

Before publishing, ask:

  • Does the first 20 seconds explain why the video exists?
  • Does the video deliver the promise in the title?
  • Can a beginner follow the steps without extra context?
  • Is the next video obvious?
  • Is the Siberian project mentioned naturally, not forced?
